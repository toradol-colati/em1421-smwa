Zachary’s Karate Club 
▶ Zachary’s karate club is a social network of a university karate club described in the paper Zachary W. (1977). An information flow model for conflict and fission in small groups. Journal of Anthropological Research, 33, 452-473 
▶ Example of how social networks can capture the sources of conflict within a group
Zachary’s Karate Club 
▶ Zachary observed 34 members of a karate club over a period of 2 years 
▶ During the course of the study, a disagreement developed between the administrator of the club and the club’s instructor ▶ In the end, the instructor left and started a new club, taking about a half of the original club’s members with him 
▶ Zachary constructed a network of friendships between members of the club, using a variety of measures to estimate the strength of ties between individuals
Zachary’s Karate Club 
▶ The instructor (node 1) and the administrator (node 34) are not friends with each other 
▶ Most people are only friends with one or the other (noninteracting clusters)

Example: Dunbar’s number 
▶ How big is a “typical” social network? 
▶ By analyzing social networks in primates, Dunbar hypothesized that there are cognitive limits to the size of one’s social network: 
▶ The limit imposed by neocortical processing capacity is simply on the number of individuals with whom a stable 
inter-personal relationship can be maintained 
▶ Dunbar estimated that the typical size of a human social network is 150 individuals
Dunbar’s number 
▶ Exchange of Christmas cards in a population of 43 families (surveys) 
▶ Trajectories data of 2,984 cards

Dunbar’s number 
▶ The average number of cards sent is 68.19 (range 11-149) ▶ Since many cards were sent by couples/families, the average size of the network is 153.5 (+-84.5)

Dunbar’s number on Twitter 
▶ In Gonçalves, Perra, & Vespignani, 2011 the authors validated Dunbar’s number for Twitter
Dunbar’s number on Twitter 
▶ In Gonçalves, Perra, & Vespignani, 2011 the authors validated Dunbar’s number for Twitter 
▶ We analyze a dataset of Twitter conversations collected across six months involving 1.7 million individuals and test the theoretical cognitive limit on the number of stable social relationships known as Dunbar’s number. We find that the data are in agreement with Dunbar’s result; users can entertain a maximum of 100–200 stable relationships. Thus, the ‘economy of attention’ is limited in the online world by cognitive and biological constraints as predicted by Dunbar’s theory.
Dunbar’s number on Twitter 
▶ On one hand, it can be argued that microblogging tools facilitate the way we handle social interactions and that this results in an online world where human social limits are finally lifted, making predictions such as the Dunbar’s number obsolete.
Dunbar’s number on Twitter

Information Networks: Divided they Blog 
▶ In 2005 Adamic and Glance measured the degree of interaction between liberal and conservative blogs 
▶ They wanted to uncover any differences in the structure of the two communities 
▶ They analyzed the posts of 1,494 blogs over the period of 2 months before the 2004 Presidential Election 
▶ They studied how often they referred to one another and quantify the overlap in the topics they discussed (intra and inter communities) 
▶ They found differences in the behavior of liberal and conservative blogs 
▶ Conservative blogs link to each other more frequently and in a denser pattern
